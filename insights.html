How Diseases Occur Together — From a Patient Graph

By turning the data of 500 patients into a graph, and without giving it any medical rules, Neo4j GDS algorithms found which diseases tend to occur together and which patients resemble each other. Below, each insight comes with its source, method, meaning and real-world use.

1. Where the data came from and what it looks like

Before doing the work, we understood the data: what it contains and how it is connected.

Source: healthcare data already loaded on the team's shared Neo4j server. It is synthetic (artificial, created for research), so there is no privacy issue for real patients.

Structure: 16 types of nodes (Patient 500, Encounter 13,115, Condition 146, Medication 111, Procedure 106, Organization 522, Payer 10 ...) and 24 types of relationships. The most important link for this work is:

Patient —HAS_CONDITION→ Condition  (4,568 links). That is, every patient is directly linked to their own diseases.

The problem was that in the graph, patients were not connected to each other and diseases were not connected to each other. The next algorithms filled exactly this gap.

2. Workflow

1Built the graphProjected Patient + Condition into GDS memory
2Computed similarityUsed Node Similarity (Jaccard) to see how much each pair has in common
3Found groupsNatural clusters using Louvain
4Extracted health meaningRead each cluster from a medical point of view

3. What the algorithms mean (in simple language)

Node Similarity (Jaccard)

How similar two things are, as a single number (0 to 1).

Jaccard = common ÷ total unique

Example: Diabetes is in 30 patients, Metabolic syndrome in 29, and 29 patients have both. Total unique is 30, common is 29, so 29/30 = 0.97.

Real use: "People like you also bought this", as on Amazon/Netflix.

Louvain (Community Detection)

Splits a network into groups so that there are many links inside a group and few outside, without being told how many groups there should be.

Analogy: at a party, people form friend groups on their own.

Real use: customer segments, fraud rings, patient cohorts.

Modularity

A score of how good Louvain's groups are. It ranges from −1 to 1, and above 0.3 means a clear structure.

Q = actual links inside groups − expected if random

Our patient groups got Q = 0.68.

Comorbidity

Medical term: two or more diseases occurring together in the same patient. It only tells you they "occur together", not "which caused which".

4. Insights

Insight 1

Diabetes doesn't come alone: a whole chain comes with it

Where it came from

Ran gds.nodeSimilarity on the Patient–Condition graph. Treated each disease as the set of its patients, then computed Jaccard between diseases. Only diseases with at least 10 patients were included.

What we found
PairPatientsCommonSimilarity
Diabetes + Metabolic syndrome X30 / 29290.97
Diabetes + Hypertriglyceridemia30 / 29290.97
CKD stage 1 + Diabetic renal disease15 / 16150.94
Of 30 diabetes patients, 29 (97%) also have metabolic syndrome and high triglycerides, and kidney disease appears to be linked with diabetes.
Real-world use
As soon as diabetes is diagnosed, a screening bundle is triggered automatically: cholesterol/triglycerides, kidney test, metabolic check. The doctor doesn't have to remember it every time.
Note: this is co-occurrence, not order. Which came first has to be checked separately using time data.
Insight 2

Pneumonia almost always comes with low oxygen

Where it came from

The same Node Similarity, the same graph. These pairs appeared at the top of the same list.

What we found
PairPatientsCommonSimilarity
Pneumonia + Hypoxemia82 / 75750.91
Pneumonia + Respiratory distress82 / 75750.91
Of 82 pneumonia patients, 75 (91%) also have low oxygen and breathing difficulty.
Real-world use
Early warning: as soon as pneumonia is diagnosed, monitor oxygen levels continuously, because here it is a signal of severity. Useful in hospital triage and ICU planning.
Note: in synthetic data, diseases are often generated in "packages", so 91% may be higher than in the real world.

Insight 4

3 natural groups of diseases, without being told

Where it came from

Built a new network of diseases: two diseases are linked when at least 10 patients have both (76 diseases, 1,126 links). Ran gds.louvain on it. Running it twice gave the same group sizes (32, 23, 10).

What we found

Group A · 32 diseases: chronic (long-lasting)

Group B · 23 diseases: mild, acute (COVID-like)

Group C · 10 diseases: serious respiratory complications

ARDSRespiratory failurePneumoniaHypoxemiaSepsisPulmonary embolismBlood clot (DVT)
Mild symptoms like cough and fever (Group B) and serious complications like ARDS and sepsis (Group C) are parts of the same COVID story, yet the algorithm kept them separate.
Real-world use
Care pathways: home care for Group B patients, hospital escalation as soon as Group C signals (oxygen, clots) appear, and a long-term program for Group A patients. Three different paths from the same data.
Note: Group A is mixed (it also picked up diseases like miscarriage and appendicitis), so call it "a big bucket of adult chronic diseases", not "one disease family". Only 76 of the 146 diseases made it into the network (because of the 10-patient condition).
Insight 5

21 natural cohorts of patients, and one metabolic cohort

Where it came from

First linked patients by the overlap of their diseases (Node Similarity, default topK = 10, meaning each patient is linked to their 10 most similar patients). Then ran Louvain on that patient network, using the similarity score as the weight.

What we found

Most common diseases in the largest group (103 patients):

Obesity · 57%Prediabetes · 47%Anemia · 41%Hypertension · 30%Heart failure · 22%Coronary heart disease · 17%Stroke · 17%
One large patient cohort has formed around the metabolic pattern of obesity → prediabetes → hypertension → heart disease.
Real-world use
Cohort-based prevention: a weight-management and BP-monitoring program for this group, since they have a higher risk of heart disease ahead.
Note: this is only a count within the group. It has not been compared with prevalence in the whole dataset, and the time order of the "chain" has not been checked. 6,289 SIMILAR_TO links were written to the server, and these include condition–condition links too (patient links are about 4,900).

5. What this means for Grasph

All of these algorithms (similarity, Louvain, centrality, PageRank, shortest path) are already implemented in Grasph's algorithms.rs and run via /api/v1/algorithms/.... That means when a customer's relationship-heavy data (patients, transactions, claims) comes into Grasph, this same analysis can run there too. One gap was spotted: Weakly Connected Components is missing (only strongly connected exists).

6. What is solid and what is still pending

Solid: the method works. Without being given any medical rules, the algorithm found familiar medical structure on its own (the diabetes chain, respiratory complications, mild vs severe). Every number came from a Neo4j query.

Cannot be claimed yet:

  • This is not causation, only co-occurrence.
  • The data is synthetic, so this is not the truth about a real population, only a demo of the method.
  • Baseline comparison (lift), time order, and significance testing are still pending.
Next step: compute the lift of these pairs, and use real timestamps to see which disease comes first.
Tools: Neo4j GDS 2026.08.1 (Node Similarity, Louvain), Python neo4j driver, Bolt protocol. Data: synthetic patient records, 500 patients.
